# Literature investigation — on-demand guide

**Purpose:** Convert existing mathematics into actionable research routes and tools, while minimizing context and reasoning overhead. Do not write a survey unless explicitly requested.

## Progressive reading

- **L1 / screen (default):** Title, abstract, precise main statements, object/field assumptions, and references. Decide whether the paper offers a direct result, a transferable technique, a constraint, or little value.
- **L2 / targeted inspection:** Read the theorem and only those definitions, lemmas, and proof passages that address a current bottleneck. Determine the mathematical mechanism, exact assumptions, and first possible application.
- **L3 / deep audit (rare):** Reconstruct a pivotal proof, its external dependencies, and its possible modification. Trigger when the theorem is to be used centrally, or the proof contains the likely breakthrough. A full proof-dependency graph is optional, not routine.

## Extract usable mathematical content

For each genuinely useful result, retain **only**:

1. Traceable source (authors, title, venue/year or identifier, theorem/proposition number, and version/page if relevant).
2. Exact mathematical statement and all assumptions that matter, including those imported through cited results.
3. Mechanism (why the proof works), not an extended paraphrase.
4. Current use: direct application, an adapted lemma to prove, an obstruction, or a proposed route.
5. What remains unverified or incompatible.

Do not treat superficially similar theorems as interchangeable. Check characteristic, field, smoothness, properness/projectivity, equivalence relation, general/special member assumptions, and any transversality conditions as relevant.

## Dynamic iteration

- Begin with direct literature; branch to methodological and citation-chain research only when it can sharpen the route inventory.
- After the initial scan, search **in response to a concrete mathematical obstruction**. Frame the search around the missing lemma or tool, not just the title of the original problem.
- After any targeted search, return to proof or construction attempts; do not accumulate sources without mathematical use.
- Stop expanding the search when the next paper has lower expected value than a concrete proof attempt. Reopen it when new evidence changes that judgment.

## Evidence labels

- **Verified literature result:** Checked in the actual source; hypotheses and conclusion recorded.
- **Literature-based deduction:** Your own valid deduction from verified source results; supply proof.
- **Proposed adaptation:** A plausible modification not yet proved.
- **Speculative direction:** Heuristic connection without adequate proof.

A search snippet or secondary citation does not alone warrant the first label. A paper's claim “no example is currently known” is not a theorem of nonexistence, and may be dated. If sources cannot be accessed, disclose the gap rather than inventing details.
